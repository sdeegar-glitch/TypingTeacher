CPCT (Computer Proficiency Certification Test) is run by Madhya Pradesh's MAP_IT and is a mandatory certification for most MP government posts involving computer work — a reusable certificate you carry into multiple recruitment cycles, not a one-time test tied to a single job. It has two typing components, English and Hindi, typed in Unicode Mangal (INSCRIPT) rather than the older Kruti Dev font layout some state exams still use. This matters because Mangal and Kruti Dev map the same physical keys to different Devanagari output, so practicing the wrong layout builds speed the actual test won't accept. For English, the consistently reported minimum is 30 net WPM. For Hindi, public sources disagree — some say 25 WPM, others 20 WPM, and at least one lists 30 WPM matching English. This isn't the requirement changing across years; it reflects genuine disagreement across third-party coaching sites, none of which is MAP_IT's own rulebook. If your Hindi speed sits anywhere in the 20-30 WPM range you're within the reported band, but confirm the exact current figure from your official CPCT rulebook or admit card before treating any single number — including the ones here — as final. CPCT scores on Net Words Per Minute: gross words (characters divided by five) minus errors, divided by minutes, then scaled into a percentage score on your certificate rather than a simple pass/fail. That means typing faster and more accurately past the bare minimum can meaningfully raise your certificate score, which some departments weight directly — a real incentive to keep improving once you've first cleared the minimum, not stop there. If you've only practiced Hindi typing on Kruti Dev for a different exam, expect an adjustment period on Mangal — the finger movements for common matras and conjuncts differ between the two layouts even though both eventually render readable Devanagari. Give yourself one to two weeks of deliberate Mangal-specific practice rather than assuming Kruti Dev speed transfers directly. A worked example makes the scoring concrete: typing 1,650 characters in 10 minutes with 15 uncorrected errors gives gross words = 1,650 ÷ 5 = 330, net words = 330 − 15 = 315, net WPM = 31.5 — comfortably above even the stricter 30 WPM reading of CPCT's English requirement. For Hindi, the same arithmetic applies, but because the pass line itself is uncertain across sources, it's worth building toward at least 32-35 WPM in Hindi regardless of which published figure you trust, so you clear whichever number your actual rulebook turns out to specify. Treating the ambiguity as a reason to practice a safer margin, rather than picking whichever number is most convenient, is the more reliable strategy here. Mistakes that cost candidates certificate score even when they technically pass: switching between Mangal and Kruti Dev muscle memory in the same practice week, which slows both; assuming a CPCT certificate from one subject area or test center transfers its exact score weighting to every department that accepts CPCT, when some roles only check pass/fail while others weight the percentage; and under-practicing Hindi relative to English because English feels more familiar, when Hindi is the component with genuine requirement uncertainty and therefore the one that most benefits from an extra margin. Since CPCT is a reusable certificate rather than a one-shot exam, it's also worth treating your first attempt as the one that sets your score for every future application that accepts it — there's a real incentive to prepare properly rather than sit for it underprepared and plan to retake it later, since re-registration and re-testing cost both time and the application fee.
