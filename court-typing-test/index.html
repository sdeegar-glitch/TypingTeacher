Court & Stenographer Typing Test — Free Practice

Free court clerk and stenographer typing test practice in Hindi and English, with live WPM and accuracy for high court & district court exams.

Court typing tests vary more by individual court and post than almost any other exam family covered on this site, so there's no single number that applies across "court clerk" or "court stenographer" generally — the specific High Court or District Judiciary running your recruitment sets its own figures. For entry-level clerk posts, official notices from courts like Himachal Pradesh High Court set 30 WPM English and 25 WPM Hindi (typed in Kruti Dev-10 font); other courts' Group C clerk tests, such as those tied to Allahabad High Court, report similar 30/25 figures over a 10-minute test. For stenographer posts at the entry level, the same Himachal Pradesh High Court notice sets a higher bar — 40 WPM on computer in English, 30 WPM in Hindi — reflecting that stenographer posts generally demand more than clerk posts even before shorthand is involved.

Shorthand-based stenographer posts add a dictation component entirely separate from typing speed: candidates take dictation at a specified speed (often 80-100 WPM for entry-level posts, higher for promotional posts) and then transcribe it on a computer within a set time limit, so raw typing speed alone doesn't determine whether you clear a stenographer skill test — transcription accuracy under time pressure matters just as much. Some promotional or senior posts, such as Personal Assistant or Judgment Writer roles, have reported typing requirements as high as 50-55 WPM, well above entry-level clerk figures — don't let a promotional-post requirement you've seen online convince you an entry-level post needs the same speed.

Accuracy penalties also vary: some courts deduct a fixed amount per mistake (0.1 marks per error has been reported for certain review-officer-style tests), while others use a straightforward pass/fail speed cutoff with a separate minimum accuracy percentage. Because of this spread, the single most useful thing you can do before practicing for a specific court recruitment is read that court's own typing-test instructions document — most High Courts publish a dedicated PDF titled something like "Instructions to Candidates — Typing Test" alongside the main recruitment notice, and it will state the exact speed, font, duration and penalty rules for that specific post.

One trap is specific to how court recruitment is organized: assuming your experience with one court's test prepares you for another's — someone who's taken a District Judiciary clerk test in one state and assumes a different state's High Court typing test uses the same speed, font or penalty system is relying on an assumption that, as shown above, frequently doesn't hold. Each court's recruitment is run independently, often by that specific High Court's own registry, and even the font requirement (Kruti Dev versus Mangal/Unicode) isn't standardized nationally the way some central-government exams are moving toward. Budget time specifically to read your court's own instructions document rather than relying on general preparation built for a different court's test.

Since no single number applies across courts, your own court's "Instructions to Candidates" PDF is the document to read before deciding what speed to train for.
