Data Entry Operator (DEO) is a post title used across many different government recruitments — SSC CGL's Tax Assistant DEST, various state data-entry cadres, and departmental DEO posts each run their own version of the test, so "the DEO typing test" isn't one standardized exam the way SSC CHSL's typing test is. What's shared across nearly all of them is the unit of measurement: key depressions per hour (KDPH) rather than words per minute, because data-entry work is measured in raw keystrokes including numbers and symbols, not natural-language words. 8,000 KDPH, the most commonly cited DEO benchmark, works out to about 133 key depressions per minute, or roughly 27 WPM at five characters per word — a useful cross-check if your practice tool reports WPM rather than KDPH directly. SSC's own Tax Assistant DEST, the closest thing to an official reference point, sets this at 2,000 key depressions over 15 minutes (the same 133/minute rate), while some other DEO-track posts have required up to 10,000 KDPH, so always confirm which specific KDPH figure applies to your post rather than assuming 8,000 is universal. Accuracy requirements for DEO posts are reported inconsistently across sources — some cite 95% as the practical target, others a lower 80% cutoff — and since no DEO-specific test I could verify officially states one universal figure, the safer approach is the same one that applies to SSC CGL DEST: practice toward 95%+ accuracy so you clear whichever cutoff actually applies, since the cost of extra accuracy practice is far lower than the cost of a failed skill test. Because DEO work is pure data entry — numbers, codes, reference IDs — rather than natural sentences, practicing exclusively on prose passages can be misleading: digit sequences and alphanumeric codes are typed differently than words, often key-by-key rather than in the recognizable chunks touch typists rely on for regular text. If your specific DEO post's skill test uses numeric or mixed data rather than a sentence passage, dedicate separate practice time to number-heavy typing drills rather than assuming your prose WPM will transfer directly. A mistake specific to DEO preparation is training purely on prose speed and assuming it predicts data-entry performance — the two skills correlate but aren't identical. Numeric and alphanumeric sequences lack the predictable letter patterns and word shapes that make prose typing faster once you're fluent; a typist who comfortably exceeds 40 WPM on sentences may find their effective rate drops noticeably on a string of account numbers or product codes, simply because there's no linguistic pattern to anticipate. If your specific DEO post's test uses this kind of data rather than sentences, build in deliberate numeric-typing practice well before relying on your prose WPM as a predictor of how you'll perform on exam day.
