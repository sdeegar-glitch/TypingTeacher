The SSC CHSL typing test is a qualifying skill test for Lower Division Clerk (LDC) and Junior Secretariat Assistant (JSA) posts, and a separate Data Entry Skill Test (DEST) for Data Entry Operator (DEO) posts — they are not the same test, and mixing up their rules is a common mistake. For the LDC/JSA typing test, candidates type for 10 minutes and must reach a minimum net speed of 35 WPM in English or 30 WPM in Hindi; you choose English or Hindi on your online application and cannot switch on exam day. The DEO-track Data Entry Skill Test instead commonly runs 15 minutes, with scribe-eligible candidates getting extra time — always confirm the exact duration for your post from the current notification at ssc.gov.in, since skill-test timing has varied slightly between recruitment cycles. Net WPM is what SSC actually scores, not raw typing speed: total characters divided by five, minus your error count, divided by minutes. A mistyped word still counts as an error even after you correct it, so the test rewards clean, careful typing over fast-but-sloppy typing — 45 WPM gross with frequent mistakes can net well under 35, while a calmer 38 WPM gross with almost no errors clears it comfortably. Backspace is allowed, but every corrected word still adds to your error tally, so minimizing how often you need to correct matters more than typing as fast as possible. The test is qualifying only — your score isn't added to your merit list, but failing it removes you from selection regardless of your Tier 1/Tier 2 marks. That makes consistency more valuable than one fast attempt: aim to clear 35 (or 30 in Hindi) comfortably on most practice attempts, not just your best one, since exam-day pressure erodes performance that calm practice sessions don't show. Here's the formula on a real attempt: suppose you type 1,850 characters in 10 minutes with 12 uncorrected errors. Gross words = 1,850 ÷ 5 = 370 words. Net words = 370 − 12 = 358. Net WPM = 358 ÷ 10 = 35.8 — a clear pass, but barely, with almost no room for a worse attempt on the actual exam day. The same candidate typing 1,650 characters (330 gross words) with only 3 errors nets 327 ÷ 10 = 32.7 — slower on paper, but further from the pass/fail boundary and more repeatable under pressure. This is why practicing for a buffer matters more than chasing a personal-best gross speed: the margin you build in practice is the margin you'll actually have on exam day, when nerves typically cost most candidates 3-5 WPM compared to their calm practice average. Common mistakes that cost candidates a pass despite adequate raw speed: typing in the wrong language because the application form's language selection wasn't double-checked before submission (unfixable after the window closes); practicing on a different Hindi layout than the one the exam center's software actually uses; rushing the first thirty seconds to "bank" speed early, which is exactly when transcription errors cluster because you haven't settled into the passage yet; and stopping practice the moment you first clear the minimum rather than continuing until clearing it feels routine. Each of these is avoidable with a few weeks' notice, which is exactly why starting typing practice as soon as you've applied — rather than waiting for the skill-test date to be announced — gives you a real margin instead of a panic sprint. Practice with a buffer: most successful candidates target 40+ WPM so an off day still clears 35. Match your practice conditions to the real test — the correct duration, your chosen language, and if typing in Hindi, the correct layout (Unicode Mangal/INSCRIPT is standard for most current SSC cycles, though this can vary — confirm from your own notification).
